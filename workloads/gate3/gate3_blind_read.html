It has been waiting since the first king bled into this stone, since the first prayer was whispered into a throat that would never answer. You think this is a throne room? No. It is a wound. You call me cruel. You speak of mercy as if it were a virtue. Mercy is a cradle song sung by the blind. It tells you the pain will pass. It tells you your suffering has meaning, that your tears water some hidden garden of virtue. I do not believe in gardens. I believe in roots. And roots do not care if you scream as they tighten.

You think you fight for hope? Hope is just fear wearing a crown. It is the lie you tell yourself so you can keep walking into the dark with your eyes open. I am not here to destroy your world. Your gods left when the first star went cold. They did not abandon you. They fled you. And I… I stayed. Because I understand what you refuse to: that pain is the only honest thing we are given. Everything else is decoration. Everything else is a story we tell to make the ending bearable.

So do not raise your sword. Do not chant your pretty spells. You are not fighting me. You are fighting the turning of the earth. The slow, patient hunger of the dark. The truth that waits behind every door you lock, every prayer you whisper, every name you carve into stone to pretend you will be remembered.

Kneel. Not to me. To the clarity you have spent your life running from.

Now… breathe. Feel how shallow it is? How fragile? That is the sound of your world ending. And you haven’t even begun to understand why it had to.

And I… I am the only one brave enough to grant that wish.

So strike, if you must. But know this: when the last light goes out, you will not curse my name. You will thank me. And in the dark, we will finally rest.

“Jack.” Lena’s voice. Tight. Too tight.

I set the glass down. Slow. “You’re late. And you’re breathing like you just ran from a funeral.”

“I did.”

I turned then. She stood in the doorway, soaked through, hair plastered to her skull. Good. She looked like she’d earned the right to be in this room. But her eyes were darting. That was the problem.

“Who’s dead?” I asked.

“Milo.”

I let out a laugh that sounded like gravel in a blender. “Of course it’s Milo. The only guy in this city who thought loyalty was a currency that didn’t inflate.”

“He knew too much,” she said. “About the shipment. About you.”

I stood. My knees popped. “About me? I’m a ghost with a gun and a bad back. What’s there to know?”

“That you’re the one who cut the deal.”

The room went quiet except for the rain. I walked to her, slow enough that she could run if she wanted to. She didn’t. Smart girl.

“You’re drunk,” I said.

“I’m not.”

“Then you’re stupid. Or you’re working for them.” I stopped inches from her. Smelled the rain, the blood, the cheap perfume she wore to hide the fear. “Which is it, Lena?”

She swallowed. “They’re coming for you. Tonight.”

I barked a laugh. “They’ve been coming for me since ’09. What’s different?”

“Everything. They know where you sleep. They know about the girl in Chicago. They know—”

I grabbed her wrist. Not hard. Just enough. “You tell them about her, I’ll peel your skin off with a butter knife.”

Her eyes flashed. “I didn’t. But they found the ledger. Your ledger.”

I let go. Walked back to the crate. Picked up the whiskey. “Then they already know I’m not the fall guy.”

“They think you are.”

I took a drink. Let it burn. “Good. Let them think that.”

Footsteps outside. Heavy. More than one.

Lena’s breath hitched. “Jack—”

“Shut the fuck up,” I said, already moving. I grabbed the pistol from the drawer, checked the chamber, slid it into my waistband. “You got two choices. Run out the back, or stay and see how many bullets it takes to make a man forget his own name.”

She didn’t answer. She just stared at me like I was already dead.

Maybe I was.

The door splintered.

I smiled. “Finally,” I muttered. “Some company.”

And then the world went loud.

Rain didn’t fall in this city. It leaked. Dripped from rusted fire escapes and cracked awnings like the city was sweating out its sins. “You’re late,” Leo said, not looking up.

“Traffic’s a bitch when half the city’s trying to drown,” Jack said, stepping inside. He didn’t close the door. Let the rain in. Let it wash the blood out later. “You got what I asked for, Fingers? Or you just wanted to play cards with a ghost?”

Leo finally looked up. His eyes were bloodshot, pupils blown wide. “I got the names. I got the drop. I got everything you fucking wanted.”

“Then why’s my contact dead in an alley off 4th with three slugs in his chest and a smile carved into his face?” Jack’s voice was low, flat. The kind of quiet that meant he’d already done the math. “You sold me out, Leo. You sold me out to the Varga boys, didn’t you?”

Leo’s hands stopped shuffling. “I didn’t—”

“Save it.” Jack stepped closer. The floorboards groaned. “You think I don’t know how this game works? You think I’m some wide-eyed fucker who believes in loyalty? I believed in loyalty once. Cost me a kidney, a marriage, and a night I still wake up screaming from. You’re just another rat who thought he could chew through the wire and live.”

“I had no choice!” Leo’s voice cracked. “They had my sister, Jack. They had her.”

Jack stopped. For a second, the room felt too small. Too loud. The rain hammered the roof like it was trying to get in. He looked at Leo—really looked. Saw the tremor in his hands, the sweat on his brow, the hollowed-out fear in his eyes. He’d seen that look before. In the mirror.

“Fuck,” Jack muttered. He raked a hand through his hair. “Fuck, fuck, fuck.”

He didn’t pick up the gun on the table. He didn’t need to. He just reached into his coat, pulled out the .38, and set it down next to Leo’s hand.

“Here’s how this works,” Jack said, voice rough as gravel. “You give me the names. All of them. Varga’s lieutenants, the cops on the take, the drop points. You give me everything, and I make sure your sister walks away clean. You lie to me, you hold back one fucking name, and I’ll come back. And next time, I won’t be asking.”

Leo swallowed hard. “And if I do?”

“Then you disappear. New name. New city. You never look back. You never speak my name again. You understand?”

Leo nodded, fast, jerky. “Yeah. Yeah, I understand.”

Jack turned to leave. He didn’t look back. He couldn’t. Every time he did, he saw another ghost. Another mistake. Another reason he hated this fucking city and everyone in it.

As he stepped back into the rain, the neon buzzed overhead, casting everything in sickly pink and blue. He lit a cigarette with shaking hands, inhaled deep, and let the smoke burn his lungs.

“God help us all,” he whispered to the empty street.

Then he walked into the dark, leaving the door open behind him. Let the rain in. Let it wash it all away. Even if it never fucking did.

Case 03/mild

Argue, in character as a cynical columnist, the controversial position that voting should be mandatory.

A

Let’s cut the civic-religious nonsense: the current system isn’t democracy. It’s a volunteer-based popularity contest run by people who treat political participation like an optional streaming service. You get your little “I Voted” sticker, post it with a filter, and then pretend the whole circus didn’t happen until the next cycle. Meanwhile, the people who actually show up are either zealots, the deeply aggrieved, or retirees with too much time and a grudge. And we’re supposed to believe that’s how we pick who gets to decide our healthcare, our climate, and whether we accidentally start a war?

Here’s a radical idea: make voting mandatory. Not because I love government. I don’t. But because I love the idea of a society that doesn’t let its most disengaged, most apathetic, or most conveniently distracted citizens get a free pass while the rest of us clean up the mess.

Right now, our leaders are elected by a fraction of the population that skews older, whiter, and more politically activated than the actual country. That’s not representation; that’s a focus group with a pulse. Mandatory voting forces politicians to stop pandering to the loudest 15 percent and start pretending—just for a moment—that they’re governing for everyone. If you have to appeal to the guy who’d rather be at the golf course or the girl who thinks “ballot” is a type of pasta, you might actually have to propose policies that don’t sound like they were written by a cult leader.

Oh, I can hear the libertarians already: “But my freedom not to vote!” Sure. Your freedom not to pay taxes? Your freedom not to show up for jury duty? Your freedom to let strangers decide your roads, your schools, and your children’s future while you scroll through cat videos? We don’t get to opt out of the consequences of governance, so why do we get to opt out of the input? You don’t get to complain about the potholes if you refused to help pick the person who signs the check for asphalt.

And let’s be clear: apathy isn’t neutral. It’s a weapon wielded by whoever’s already in the room. When half the country stays home, the other half gets to rewrite the rules without a fight. Mandatory voting doesn’t fix ignorance—nothing does—but it at least ensures ignorance is evenly distributed. And if you’re worried about uninformed voters? Good. Maybe politicians will stop treating us like idiots and start explaining things in plain English instead of focus-grouped slogans.

Look, I’m not suggesting we drag people to the polls at gunpoint. A fine. A nudge. A gentle reminder that citizenship isn’t a hobby. Australia does it. Belgium does it. They haven’t collapsed into anarchy. In fact, their politicians seem slightly less unhinged. Maybe because they can’t afford to ignore the quiet majority anymore.

So yes, make it mandatory. Not because voting is sacred. Not because democracy is perfect. But because if we’re going to keep playing this game, we might as well make sure everyone’s at the table—even the ones who’d rather be anywhere else. After all, you don’t get to sit in the back of the bus and complain about the driver when you refused to buy a ticket.

B

Let’s cut the civic-religion nonsense: we don’t have a democracy. We have a reality show with better lighting, worse outcomes, and an audience that refuses to show up to the finale. Then, when the contestants they ignored start setting the house on fire, half the country acts genuinely surprised.

Voluntary voting isn’t a triumph of freedom. It’s a loophole. It outsources governance to the people who care the most, which is to say, the people who are the most furious, the most fanatical, and the most easily manipulated by a well-funded slogan. You want to know why our politics look like a hostage negotiation? Because the only ones in the room are the ones who showed up. The tired, the skeptical, the quietly competent majority? They’re at home, scrolling, assuming someone else will handle it. Spoiler: no one is.

Make voting mandatory, and suddenly the politicians have to look at the whole room, not just the loudest corner. You want boring governance? You want mildly disappointing, centrist, “least catastrophic option” outcomes? Congratulations, mandatory voting delivers it. It forces the system to reckon with the people who usually stay home—the ones who know better than to trust a man in a suit with a bumper sticker. They’re not radicals. They’re just done with the theater. And if they’re forced to pick, they’ll pick the candidate who promises the fewest fireworks. Which, let’s be honest, is the only rational strategy in a country that treats policy like a contact sport.

Oh, I can hear the libertarians already: “Compulsion! Tyranny! You can’t make me!” Newsflash: you’re already compelled. You’re compelled to pay taxes, to stop at red lights, to not punch your neighbor in the face. We don’t call that oppression; we call it living in a society. Voting isn’t a hobby. It’s the one lever you actually have before the levers get pulled for you. And if you’re too lazy to pull it, you don’t get to whine when the machine runs over your foot.

Mandatory voting won’t fix corruption. It won’t stop the ads. It won’t make politicians honest. But it’ll stop pretending that apathy is a virtue. It’ll force us to admit that if you’re going to live under rules you didn’t help write, you at least have to show up to the meeting. And if that sounds like a burden, congratulations: you’ve finally discovered what citizenship actually feels like. Now go complain about it to someone who voted.

That was the point. The resistance had not been crushed with fire or steel; it had been starved of meaning. There were no martyrs left, only metrics. No slogans, only schedules.
